## 1. Sources and Routes of Exposure

### 1.1 Methanol

Methanol is a ubiquitous industrial and consumer solvent. MedlinePlus lists its presence in "antifreeze, canned heating sources, copy machine fluids, de-icing fluid, fuel additives, paint removers, shellac, varnish, and windshield wiper fluid" ([MedlinePlus, *Methanol poisoning*](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/002680.htm)).

Clinically important exposure contexts in adults:

- **Automotive and household products** — windshield washer fluid and gas-line antifreeze/de-icer are the most common domestic sources, and are often high-concentration.
- **Surrogate and adulterated alcohol** — methanol substituted for or contaminating beverage ethanol. This is the mechanism of the large outbreak clusters, and it is the setting in which a poison center is most likely to face multiple simultaneous patients. `[CITATION NEEDED — cite a specific CDC/MMWR outbreak report; verify via search of MMWR for methanol outbreak investigations rather than citing generically]`
- **Occupational solvent use** — paint stripping, varnishing, duplicating fluids.
- **Deliberate self-poisoning**, and inadvertent ingestion from decanting into unlabelled drink containers.

**Routes.** Ingestion is the dominant route for serious systemic toxicity. Methanol is also absorbed across skin and through inhalation of vapour in sufficient quantity to cause systemic toxicity in occupational settings — a distinction from ethylene glycol, and a reason to take dermal and inhalational histories seriously in workplace exposures. `[CITATION NEEDED — PubChem CID 887 / HSDB record for route-specific absorption]`

### 1.2 Ethylene Glycol

Ethylene glycol is principally encountered as engine coolant/antifreeze, and additionally in brake and hydraulic fluids, de-icing fluids, and some industrial solvents and heat-transfer fluids. `[CITATION NEEDED — PubChem CID 174 / HSDB record for product occurrence]`

Clinically important exposure contexts in adults:

- **Deliberate self-poisoning** — the single most common serious adult presentation.
- **Ethanol substitution** — ingested as a beverage surrogate; it is sweet-tasting, which removes the aversive cue that limits many caustic ingestions.
- **Inadvertent ingestion** from decanted or unlabelled containers.
- **Rare malicious administration** — consider in unexplained high-anion-gap acidosis with renal failure.

**Routes.** CDC/NIOSH is explicit that systemic toxicity is an ingestion phenomenon: inhalation of vapour "is unlikely to cause systemic toxicity," dermal absorption is minimal, and ocular exposure produces local effects only ([CDC/NIOSH, *Ethylene Glycol: Systemic Agent*](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/ershdb/emergencyresponsecard_29750031.html)). Practically: an adult with systemic ethylene glycol toxicity has swallowed it.

## 2. Mechanism of Toxicity

The governing principle for both agents: **the parent alcohol is responsible for intoxication; the metabolites are responsible for death and permanent injury.** Both are substrates for alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH), and the entire antidotal strategy follows from this.

### 2.1 Methanol

**Metabolic sequence.** Methanol → (ADH) → formaldehyde → (aldehyde dehydrogenase) → **formic acid / formate**. Formaldehyde is transient and not the clinically relevant toxin; formate is.

**Formate as the effector.** Formate inhibits mitochondrial cytochrome *c* oxidase, producing cellular-level histotoxic hypoxia and metabolic acidosis. Acidosis is thus doubly driven — by formate as a titratable acid, and by the lactate generated when oxidative phosphorylation is blocked. Falling pH increases the undissociated formic acid fraction, which crosses membranes more readily, so acidosis accelerates tissue penetration. This positive feedback is why methanol poisoning deteriorates nonlinearly and why acidosis must be corrected aggressively. `[CITATION NEEDED — StatPearls NBK482121 for the formate/cytochrome oxidase mechanism and the acidosis–penetration feedback]`

**Target organs.**

| Target | Injury | Clinical correlate |
|---|---|---|
| **Retina / optic nerve** | Formate inhibits oxidative phosphorylation in retinal and optic nerve tissue; demyelination of the optic nerve | Blurred vision, central scotoma, "snowfield" vision, photophobia, afferent pupillary defect, blindness |
| **Basal ganglia (putamen)** | Haemorrhagic/necrotic infarction, characteristically bilateral putaminal | Extrapyramidal sequelae, parkinsonism in survivors |
| **CNS (global)** | Cerebral oedema, depression | Headache, confusion, coma, seizures |
| **Cardiovascular** | Acidosis-driven myocardial depression, vasodilation | Hypotension, shock |

Visual toxicity is the signature of methanol and must be documented on arrival and serially. MedlinePlus notes that "blindness is common and often permanent despite medical care" ([MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/002680.htm)) — accurate for untreated or late-presenting patients, and the reason early antidote matters more than any other single intervention.

### 2.2 Ethylene Glycol

**Metabolic sequence.** Ethylene glycol → (ADH) → glycolaldehyde → (aldehyde dehydrogenase) → **glycolic acid** → glyoxylic acid → **oxalic acid**.

**Division of labour between metabolites:**

- **Glycolic acid** is the principal cause of the high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis and correlates best with acidosis severity and outcome.
- **Oxalic acid** precipitates with calcium as **calcium oxalate**, producing direct renal tubular injury, crystalluria, and **hypocalcaemia** by sequestration.

`[CITATION NEEDED — StatPearls NBK448167 for the glycolate-vs-oxalate division and the glycolate/outcome correlation]`

**Acid–base effects (both agents).** Expect:

- **Early:** elevated **osmolal gap** from the unmetabolised parent alcohol, with a normal or near-normal anion gap.
- **Late:** elevated **anion gap** metabolic acidosis from accumulated organic acid, with a *narrowing* osmolal gap as the parent is consumed.

This reciprocal relationship is the chapter's most useful diagnostic concept. A late-presenting patient may have a normal osmolal gap and still be gravely poisoned; a very early patient may have a normal anion gap and still require antidote. **Neither gap alone excludes toxic alcohol ingestion.** Note also that a normal osmolal gap is unreliable in isolation because the reference range is wide and an individual's baseline is unknown.

**Target organs (ethylene glycol).** CDC/NIOSH describes the sequence plainly: ethylene glycol and its toxic byproducts "first affect the central nervous system (CNS), then the heart, and finally the kidneys" ([CDC/NIOSH](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/ershdb/emergencyresponsecard_29750031.html)). The kidney is the organ of lasting morbidity; cranial neuropathies are a described late complication.

## 3. Pharmacokinetics

| Parameter | Methanol | Ethylene Glycol |
|---|---|---|
| Absorption | Rapid from GI tract | Rapid from GI tract |
| Peak concentration | Within ~30–90 min `[CITATION NEEDED]` | Within ~1–4 h `[CITATION NEEDED]` |
| Volume of distribution | ~0.6–0.7 L/kg; distributes into total body water `[CITATION NEEDED]` | ~0.6–0.8 L/kg; distributes into total body water `[CITATION NEEDED]` |
| Protein binding | Negligible | Negligible |
| Primary elimination | Hepatic metabolism via ADH (saturable, approximately zero-order at toxic concentrations) | Hepatic metabolism via ADH; parent also renally excreted unchanged |
| Half-life, untreated | Prolonged and dose-dependent `[CITATION NEEDED — StatPearls NBK482121]` | Approximately 3 h `[CITATION NEEDED — StatPearls NBK448167]` |
| Half-life, ADH blocked (fomepizole/ethanol) | Markedly prolonged — elimination becomes dependent on slow renal/pulmonary clearance of parent | Prolonged to ~15–20 h, shifting to renal excretion of parent `[CITATION NEEDED]` |
| Half-life, haemodialysis | Short — on the order of a few hours `[CITATION NEEDED]` | Short — on the order of 2–3 h `[CITATION NEEDED]` |

**Three kinetic consequences that drive management:**

1. **Low Vd, no protein binding, low molecular weight → both agents and their acid metabolites are efficiently dialysable.** Haemodialysis is definitive therapy, not a salvage measure.
2. **Blocking ADH prolongs parent half-life substantially.** Fomepizole stops the production of toxin but does not remove the reservoir. A patient with a large body burden who is blocked but not dialysed must be observed for a long period, and the duration of antidote is governed by serial concentrations, not a fixed course.
3. **Co-ingested ethanol is a competitive ADH substrate and delays toxic metabolite formation.** It can postpone acidosis by many hours and produce a deceptively well patient whose deterioration begins as ethanol clears. Always ask about, and measure, co-ingested ethanol.

**Latent period.** Because toxicity is metabolite-dependent, there is a characteristic interval between ingestion and severe illness — and the interval is longest in exactly the patients who look best initially. Treat the history, not the current appearance.

## 4. Dose–Response and Risk Assessment

### 4.1 Framing

The clinically actionable question is **not** "how much is fatal?" It is **"has this patient crossed the threshold at which antidotal therapy is indicated?"** The threshold sits far below any lethality estimate, and management is driven by that threshold plus serum concentration and acid–base status.

### 4.2 Minimum toxic dose / threshold for antidotal therapy

**Ethylene glycol.** Ingestion exceeding approximately **0.1 mL/kg of the pure substance** is the conventional threshold above which a patient is considered at risk and treated. For a 60–80 kg adult this corresponds to a small fraction of a mouthful of concentrated product — operationally, **any credible swallow of concentrated antifreyze should be treated as threshold-crossing** pending concentrations. `[CITATION NEEDED — verify 0.1 mL/kg against StatPearls NBK448167 and Barceloux et al., AACT practice guideline]`

**Methanol.** The minimum dose associated with irreversible optic nerve injury is reported in the low-millilitre range for an adult — a quantity well within a single incidental swallow of a high-concentration product. Visual loss can therefore occur after ingestions that a patient describes as trivial, which is the central risk-assessment point for methanol. `[CITATION NEEDED — verify the minimum ocular-toxic dose against StatPearls NBK482121 and PubChem CID 887/HSDB]`

### 4.3 Treat-regardless criteria

Independent of reported volume, initiate ADH blockade and involve nephrology and the poison center for **any** of the following:

- Serum methanol or ethylene glycol concentration **≥ 20 mg/dL** (conventional treatment threshold) `[CITATION NEEDED — confirm 20 mg/dL in both StatPearls chapters]`
- **Documented or strongly suspected ingestion** of a toxic alcohol **plus** either an osmolal gap > 10 mOsm/kg or an unexplained high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis
- **Any intentional self-poisoning** history involving a toxic-alcohol-containing product
- **Arterial pH < 7.3**, bicarbonate < 20 mmol/L, or rising anion gap
- **Visual symptoms** (methanol) or **oxalate crystalluria / rising creatinine** (ethylene glycol)
- **Altered mental status** with unexplained acidosis and no alternative explanation

Reported volume is the weakest item on this list. It should never be the reason therapy is withheld.

### 4.4 Why volume-based triage fails

Four independent reasons the reported millilitre figure cannot be trusted:

1. **Histories are unreliable.** Self-reported volumes in intentional ingestion are inaccurate in both directions; co-ingestants and altered mentation degrade them further; bystander estimates of "a mouthful" vary several-fold.
2. **Product concentration varies widely** by brand, region, formulation, and reformulation, and labels are frequently absent, decanted, or wrong. The same nominal volume may differ severalfold in dose.
3. **Co-ingested ethanol shifts the entire timeline**, decoupling the dose from the clinical picture at the moment of assessment.
4. **Measured concentration and acid–base status are available and superior.** When a direct measure of body burden and physiological derangement exists, an estimate derived from a disputed volume and an assumed concentration adds nothing and can mislead toward under-treatment.

### 4.5 Published lethality — prose statement

Both methanol and ethylene glycol are lethal after ingestion of modest volumes of concentrated consumer product, and untreated poisoning by either agent is frequently fatal — CDC/NIOSH states that untreated ethylene glycol poisoning "can be fatal" ([CDC/NIOSH](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/ershdb/emergencyresponsecard_29750031.html)), and MedlinePlus describes methanol as dangerous in small quantities with prognosis dependent on the amount ingested and the speed of treatment ([MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/002680.htm)).

The clinically important counterpoint: **with timely ADH blockade and haemodialysis, survival with intact organ function is expected even at concentrations historically regarded as lethal.** Outcome in the modern era is determined far more by time-to-antidote and time-to-dialysis than by ingested dose. Late presentation with established acidosis — not a large reported volume — is the adverse prognostic finding. `[CITATION NEEDED — support the outcome-vs-time-to-treatment claim from StatPearls NBK482121 / NBK448167 or an outbreak case series]`

## 5. Onset and Clinical Course

### 5.1 Methanol

| Phase | Timing | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Early / intoxication** | 0–1 h | Inebriation (often milder than expected for the apparent dose), nausea, vomiting, gastritis |
| **Latent** | ~1–12 h, may extend to ~24 h or longer with co-ingested ethanol | Deceptive improvement; patient may appear well or be discharged in error |
| **Metabolic / toxic** | ~12–24 h, sometimes later | High-anion-gap acidosis, hyperventilation (Kussmaul), visual disturbance, headache, abdominal pain |
| **Severe / terminal** | Progressive | Coma, seizures, cerebral oedema, blindness, bradycardia, hypotension, death |

Visual symptoms arising within roughly 12 hours of exposure are a red flag demanding immediate escalation. `[CITATION NEEDED — timing of visual onset, StatPearls NBK482121]`

**Note on odour and intoxication:** methanol produces less apparent inebriation per unit of intoxication than ethanol, so a patient who seems "not that drunk" is not reassuring.

### 5.2 Ethylene Glycol

CDC/NIOSH frames the course in three stages, with the timings below taken directly from that source ([CDC/NIOSH](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/ershdb/emergencyresponsecard_29750031.html)):

| Stage | Timing (CDC/NIOSH) | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — Neurological** | "30 minutes to 12 hours after ingestion" | Intoxication *without the odour of ethanol*, euphoria, ataxia, slurred speech, nausea and vomiting; progressing in severe cases to CNS depression, seizures, coma, respiratory depression |
| **2 — Cardiopulmonary** | "between 12 and 24 hours after ingestion" | Tachycardia, tachypnoea/hyperventilation, hypertension, worsening metabolic acidosis; in severe cases cardiac failure and pulmonary oedema. **Most untreated deaths occur in this window.** |
| **3 — Renal** | "between 24 and 72 hours after ingestion" | Flank pain, oliguria, acute kidney injury from calcium oxalate deposition, hypocalcaemia; acute renal failure |

Apparent intoxication **without** the smell of alcohol is the classic early clue. Late cranial neuropathy is a recognised fourth-phase complication. `[CITATION NEEDED — late cranial neuropathy, StatPearls NBK448167]`

## 6. Management

### 6.1 Immediate priorities

1. **Airway, breathing, circulation.** Intubate for coma or inability to protect the airway. Resuscitate hypotension with isotonic crystalloid.
2. **Call the poison center — 1-800-222-1222 —** at the point of suspicion. Early discussion changes antidote and dialysis timing, and in outbreak settings identifies clustering.
3. **Do not delay antidote for confirmatory concentrations.** Toxic alcohol assays are frequently unavailable or slow. Treat on suspicion and de-escalate later.

### 6.2 Decontamination

- **Activated charcoal does not meaningfully adsorb either alcohol** and is not indicated for isolated toxic alcohol ingestion. It retains a role only for a relevant co-ingestant with an appropriate time window and a protected airway. (MedlinePlus lists charcoal among general emergency measures; in isolated toxic alcohol ingestion it offers no benefit.) `[CITATION NEEDED — confirm lack of charcoal binding, StatPearls]`
- **Gastric lavage** is not routinely indicated; absorption is too rapid for it to be useful in most presentations.

### 6.3 Antidote — ADH blockade

**Fomepizole is the agent of choice** for both poisonings. CDC/NIOSH confirms that "fomepizole and ethanol are effective antidotes against ethylene glycol toxicity" and should be given "as soon as possible once the patient/victim is admitted to a medical care facility" ([CDC/NIOSH](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/ershdb/emergencyresponsecard_29750031.html)); MedlinePlus lists fomepizole and ethanol as the antidotes for methanol ([MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/002680.htm)).

- **Fomepizole dosing:** `[CITATION NEEDED — do not release without verified loading dose, maintenance dose, dosing interval, the increased frequency required during haemodialysis, and endpoint criteria. Verify against StatPearls NBK482121/NBK448167 and the product label. This handbook should carry the dose only once a pharmacist has signed it off.]`
- **Ethanol** is the alternative where fomepizole is unavailable, exploiting ethanol's far higher ADH affinity. It requires a continuous infusion, frequent ethanol-level monitoring to hold a target concentration, and management of consequent CNS depression, hypoglycaemia (particularly relevant in malnourished or alcohol-dependent patients), and the need for a central line. It is materially harder to run safely than fomepizole. `[CITATION NEEDED — target serum ethanol concentration and infusion regimen]`
- **Endpoint:** continue blockade until the toxic alcohol concentration is below the treatment threshold **and** acidosis has resolved. Do not stop on clinical appearance alone.

### 6.4 Haemodialysis

Haemodialysis removes both parent alcohol and acid metabolites, and CDC/NIOSH describes it as "the most effective form of treatment for an acidotic patient/victim" ([CDC/NIOSH](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/ershdb/emergencyresponsecard_29750031.html)). MedlinePlus likewise notes for methanol that "because rapid removal of methanol is a key to treatment success and survival, the person will likely need dialysis" ([MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/002680.htm)).

**Indications — consult nephrology early for:**

- Significant metabolic acidosis (pH < 7.3) or refractory acidosis
- End-organ toxicity: visual impairment (methanol), acute kidney injury (ethylene glycol)
- High measured serum concentration `[CITATION NEEDED — concentration-based dialysis threshold]`
- Clinical deterioration despite ADH blockade and supportive care
- Electrolyte derangement unresponsive to standard therapy

**Operational point:** fomepizole is itself dialysable, so dosing frequency must increase during dialysis. Confirm the adjusted regimen with pharmacy when the circuit starts.

### 6.5 Adjunctive therapy

| Agent | Indication | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| **Sodium bicarbonate** | Metabolic acidosis | Raises pH, reducing the diffusible undissociated acid fraction and tissue penetration; enhances renal formate/glycolate elimination. Dose to pH, with close monitoring of potassium and ionised calcium. `[CITATION NEEDED — target pH and dosing]` |
| **Folate / folinic acid (leucovorin)** | **Methanol** | Accelerates formate oxidation to carbon dioxide. CDC/NIOSH lists folinic acid among supportive agents; dose `[CITATION NEEDED]` |
| **Thiamine and pyridoxine** | **Ethylene glycol** | Shunt glyoxylate toward less toxic metabolites rather than oxalate. CDC/NIOSH lists thiamine and pyridoxine as supporting metabolism ([CDC/NIOSH](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/ershdb/emergencyresponsecard_29750031.html)); dose `[CITATION NEEDED]` |
| **Calcium** | **Symptomatic** hypocalcaemia only (tetany, seizures, arrhythmia) | Replacing calcium in asymptomatic hypocalcaemia may increase calcium oxalate precipitation and worsen renal injury. Treat the symptom, not the number. `[CITATION NEEDED]` |

### 6.6 Monitoring and disposition

- **Serial** arterial or venous blood gas, electrolytes with calculated anion gap, osmolality with calculated osmolal gap, renal function, and — where available — toxic alcohol concentrations.
- Urinalysis for calcium oxalate crystals in suspected ethylene glycol. **Absence of crystals does not exclude poisoning.** Urine fluorescence from antifreeze additive is neither sensitive nor specific and should not guide decisions.
- **Formal visual acuity and fundoscopic assessment** on arrival and serially in methanol exposure; involve ophthalmology for any visual symptom.
- Co-ingestant screen including ethanol, paracetamol/acetaminophen and salicylate in intentional ingestion.
- **Observation:** a patient with a credible toxic alcohol ingestion who is asymptomatic with normal gaps still requires a period of observation covering the latent interval, extended if ethanol was co-ingested. Discharge during the latent period is a recognised and preventable error.
- Psychiatric assessment before discharge after intentional ingestion.
- Document the product, concentration if available, and time of ingestion, and report to the poison center — case data supports outbreak detection.

## 7. Pitfalls

1. **Discharging a well patient during the latent period.** The commonest serious error in both poisonings.
2. **Relying on a normal osmolal gap to exclude ingestion.** Wide reference range, unknown individual baseline, and it narrows as the parent is metabolised.
3. **Relying on a normal anion gap in a very early presentation.** The acidosis has not yet been generated.
4. **Withholding antidote pending a confirmatory concentration.** Treat on suspicion.
5. **Being reassured by co-ingested ethanol.** It buys time; it does not reduce the eventual burden.
6. **Repleting asymptomatic hypocalcaemia in ethylene glycol poisoning.** Risks worsening oxalate deposition.
7. **Failing to increase fomepizole frequency during haemodialysis.**
8. **Expecting the smell of alcohol.** Its absence in an apparently intoxicated patient points *toward* a toxic alcohol.
