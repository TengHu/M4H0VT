### Findings worth flagging for the article

1. **99% isopropyl alcohol is not part of the drugstore first-aid set.** A survey of Target's isopropyl category and CVS's rubbing-alcohol listings turned up **no 99% bottled product at either retailer**. 99% IPA is generally sold as an electronics/industrial cleaner rather than an OTC antiseptic — it is not an FDA OTC first-aid monograph concentration the way 70% and 91% are. If the article implies a 70/91/99 lineup on drugstore shelves, that framing is wrong.

2. **Target carries essentially no name-brand bottled rubbing alcohol.** Every bottled liquid in Target's isopropyl results was up&up. The only non-store brands present were prep pads, not bottles: DMI Large Alcohol Prep Pads 70%, 100 ct, $12.99, and CURAD Sterile Isopropyl Prep Pads, 200 ct, $8.63 ([target.com search](https://www.target.com/s?searchTerm=isopropyl+alcohol)). Name brands in this category (Swan, Equate) show up mainly at Walmart; I found **no confirmed Swan listing at Target or CVS**. Don't assert name-brand availability at these two retailers without an in-store check.

3. **Appearance is not a differentiator within this category.** Unscented 70% and 91% isopropyl alcohol are both clear and colorless, in both store brands. Concentration is not visually distinguishable — the only reliable indicator is the label. This matters for the storage-safety section below.

## Storage safety: the automotive and household chemicals that don't belong near this shelf

This section covers methanol products (gas-line antifreeze/de-icer, windshield washer fluid) and ethylene glycol engine coolant. It is written as storage guidance, not as a product comparison, and deliberately **does not** profile these products by how their contents look. The reason is the central safety point below.

### Appearance is not a safety cue — which is why the original-container rule is absolute

The honest answer to "what does it look like on the shelf" is: **it does not reliably matter, and relying on it is the failure mode.** Automotive chemicals are sold in a range of colors and tints, some of them water-clear, and the product's appearance tells a person nothing about its toxicity. Once any of these liquids leaves its labeled container, there is no dependable way for the next person — a child, a houseguest, a family member, a first responder — to identify it.

So the rule for the article is not "learn to spot them." It is:

- **Never decant these into a drink container.** Water bottles, sports-drink bottles, soda bottles, and travel mugs are the single most common route to serious accidental poisoning with household and automotive chemicals. A bottle that reads "spring water" will be trusted by someone who did not fill it.
- **Never transfer them into unlabeled jugs, jars, or spray bottles**, even in a garage. Keep the manufacturer's container and label, with its hazard warnings and first-aid instructions intact — emergency clinicians and Poison Control need the product name and ingredients to treat correctly.
- **Keep them physically separated from food, drink, and medicine.** Not on pantry or kitchen shelves, not in the refrigerator, and not alongside the rubbing alcohol and peroxide in a bathroom cabinet. Store automotive chemicals in the garage or a utility space, high up or locked, away from children and pets.
- **Re-cap fully and clean up spills immediately**, especially coolant spills on a garage floor or driveway, which pets will investigate.

### The sweetness problem (ethylene glycol)

Ethylene glycol — the active ingredient in conventional engine antifreeze/coolant — is both **sweet-tasting and highly toxic**, a combination that makes it uniquely dangerous around children and animals. Unlike most household poisons, taste gives no warning and may actively invite a second swallow. Animal-welfare estimates cited in the 2012 bitterant campaign put US pet poisonings in the range of **10,000 to 90,000 animals per year**, with children also at risk ([Humane Society Legislative Fund](https://humaneaction.org/press-release/2012/12/antifreeze-and-engine-coolant-being-bittered-nationwide)).

Methanol products (gas-line antifreeze such as HEET, windshield washer fluid) carry a different profile worth a sentence in the article: methanol is severely toxic by ingestion, with a characteristic **delay between exposure and symptoms**, and a small volume can cause permanent blindness or death. Methanol is also absorbed through skin and inhaled as vapor, so these are not products to use in an unventilated space or to handle casually.

For both ethylene glycol and methanol, the delayed onset is the reason not to wait and see. Effective antidotal treatment exists and works best when started early.

### What bitterant labeling does — and does not — cover

This is the part consumer articles usually get wrong, so it's worth stating precisely.

**What happened:** In December 2012, the Consumer Specialty Products Association and the Humane Society Legislative Fund announced a **voluntary industry agreement** to add the bitter agent **denatonium benzoate** to antifreeze and engine coolant sold to consumers in all 50 states and DC. Before that, **17 states** had already mandated it by law (Arizona, California, Georgia, Illinois, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, New Jersey, New Mexico, Oregon, Tennessee, Utah, Vermont, Virginia, Washington, West Virginia, Wisconsin); repeated attempts at a federal mandate had failed ([HSLF](https://humaneaction.org/press-release/2012/12/antifreeze-and-engine-coolant-being-bittered-nationwide), [dvm360](https://dvm360.com/view/antifreeze-manufacturers-agree-add-bittering-agent-make-products-unpalatable-animals-and-humans)).

**What it does not do:**

- **A bitterant reduces appeal, not toxicity.** The chemistry is unchanged; a bittered product that is swallowed is exactly as dangerous as an unbittered one. As one veterinary summary put it, antifreeze got "safer, but not safe" ([petMD](https://www.petmd.com/blogs/fullyvetted/2013/feb/antifreeze-just-got-safer-with-bitter-29817)).
- **It is not a guarantee of deterrence.** Bitterness can be overridden — by a determined toddler, by an animal, by anyone who has already begun swallowing, and by dilution in another liquid.
- **Coverage is incomplete.** The 2012 agreement was voluntary and aimed at consumer-market antifreeze and engine coolant. It does not blanket every automotive or household chemical category, and it does not reach products outside the consumer channel.
- **The label is not a safety rating.** Readers should not treat the presence or absence of a bitterant mention as a signal of how cautiously to store something. Every product in this group should be stored as if nothing in it will stop a child or pet who gets the cap off, because functionally, nothing will.

The takeaway for the article: bitterants are a backstop against one specific accident — tasting and continuing — and they are a real public-health win on that narrow axis. They are not a substitute for a locked cabinet, a labeled original container, and keeping these liquids off the shelf where food and first-aid supplies live.

### If an exposure happens

Call **Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222** (US) or use [poison.org](https://www.poison.org) immediately — do not wait for symptoms, and do not wait to see whether the person or animal seems fine, because both ethylene glycol and methanol have a symptom-free interval during which treatment is most effective. Have the container with you so you can read the product name and ingredients. For animals, contact a veterinarian or an animal poison control line at once. Call emergency services for anyone who is unconscious, seizing, or having trouble breathing.
