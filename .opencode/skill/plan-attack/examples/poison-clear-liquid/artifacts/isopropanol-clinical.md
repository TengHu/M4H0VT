# Isopropanol (Isopropyl Alcohol, 2-Propanol)

**CAS 67-63-0 · PubChem CID 3776 · MW 60.1 g/mol · density 0.79 g/mL**
**Conversion:** 1 mg/dL = 0.166 mmol/L (100 mg/dL ≈ 16.6 mmol/L)

> **Key points**
> - A CNS depressant roughly twice as potent as ethanol, with a long-lived CNS-active metabolite (acetone). Hallmark: **osmol gap and ketosis *without* anion-gap acidosis**.
> - Potentially lethal adult dose: **2–4 mL/kg**, i.e. roughly **150–350 mL (5–12 fl oz) of 70% rubbing alcohol** for a 70-kg adult. Survival after larger ingestions is well documented with supportive care.
> - Treatment is supportive. **Fomepizole and ethanol are not indicated** and prolong intoxication.
> - Hemodialysis is rarely needed; consider for **refractory hypotension**, deep coma/respiratory failure not manageable with support, or serum level **> 400–500 mg/dL**.
> - A significant anion-gap acidosis is *not* explained by isopropanol alone: look for a co-ingestant (methanol, ethylene glycol), lactate from shock, or another cause.

Items marked **†** come from standard secondary references that could not be opened for direct verification during drafting; see [Verification notes](#verification-notes) before publication.

## 1. Sources and routes of exposure

- **Products:** rubbing alcohol (typically 70%, up to 99% in some products), hand sanitizers, alcohol swabs, cleaners, solvents, perfumes ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/); [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/002660.htm)). Alcohol-based hand sanitizers contain 60–95% ethanol or isopropanol by volume ([CDC MMWR 2017](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/66/wr/mm6608a5.htm)).
- **Epidemiology:** the most common toxic-alcohol exposure reported to US poison centers; > 80% of reported cases are unintentional. Intentional cases are usually ethanol substitution ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/)).
- **Routes:** ingestion predominates. **Dermal and inhalational absorption cause systemic toxicity**, classically in febrile children sponged with rubbing alcohol; deep coma has resulted ([PubChem/HSDB](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/3776); [poison.org](https://www.poison.org/articles/rubbing-alcohol-only-looks-like-water)).

## 2. Mechanism of toxicity

| Effect | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| CNS depression | Parent compound is a direct CNS depressant, more intoxicating than ethanol (higher molecular weight, lower polarity) and about twice as toxic ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/); [PubChem/HSDB](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/3776)). The metabolite **acetone** is also a CNS depressant and, with its long half-life, prolongs sedation ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/)). |
| Ketosis without acidosis | Alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) oxidizes a secondary alcohol to a ketone, not an acid. Acetone is uncharged, so there is ketonemia/ketonuria with no anion-gap acidosis, in contrast to methanol and ethylene glycol ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/)). |
| GI injury | Direct mucosal irritant: vomiting, abdominal pain, hemorrhagic gastritis, hematemesis ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/); [poison.org](https://www.poison.org/articles/rubbing-alcohol-only-looks-like-water)). |
| Cardiovascular | Hypotension in severe overdose, attributed to peripheral vasodilation and myocardial depression†; a marker of severe poisoning ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/)). |

## 3. Pharmacokinetics

| Parameter | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Absorption | ~80% within 30 min; complete within 2 h | [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/); [PubChem/HSDB](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/3776) |
| Peak serum level | 30 min – 3 h post-ingestion | [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/) |
| Volume of distribution | 0.6–0.7 L/kg (total body water); tissue distribution complete by ~2 h | [PubChem/HSDB](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/3776) |
| Metabolism | Hepatic ADH → acetone; first-order kinetics; ~80% of absorbed dose metabolized | [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/) |
| Unchanged excretion | 20–50% renal in older series; some exhaled | [PubChem/HSDB](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/3776) |
| Half-life, isopropanol | 3–7 h (reported range 2.5–8 h; longer with ethanol co-ingestion, shorter in chronic alcohol users) | [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/); [PubChem/HSDB](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/3776) |
| Half-life, acetone | ~22 h; eliminated by kidney and lung | [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/); [PubChem/HSDB](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/3776) |

**Clinical consequences of the kinetics**

- Absorption is too fast for GI decontamination to help.
- A patient who is going to become seriously ill declares it early: those who have **not developed coma within 6 h of ingestion are unlikely to need extracorporeal removal** ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/)).
- The isopropanol level falls while acetone rises; sedation, ketosis and acetone breath can persist a day or more after the parent compound is gone.
- Ethanol co-ingestion competes for ADH and prolongs the isopropanol half-life ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/)).

## 4. Dose–response

### 4.1 Ingested dose

Volumes below are for **70% (v/v) product**, which contains about 0.55 g isopropanol per mL. Scale for other strengths (91% ≈ 0.72 g/mL; 99% ≈ 0.78 g/mL).

| Dose of 70% product | 70-kg adult | Expected effect | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Taste / sip | < ~10 mL | Few or no symptoms; oral and gastric irritation | [poison.org](https://www.poison.org/articles/rubbing-alcohol-only-looks-like-water) |
| ~0.5–1 mL/kg | 35–70 mL | Threshold for clinically evident intoxication† | Olson† |
| **2–4 mL/kg** | **140–280 mL** | **Potentially lethal:** coma, respiratory depression, hypotension | [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/) |
| ~250 mL | — | Classically cited adult fatal dose | [PubChem/HSDB](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/3776) (Dreisbach) |
| 190 g isopropanol (≈ 240 mL pure ≈ 350 mL of 70%) | — | "Probable lethal oral dose" | [CDC/NIOSH IDLH](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/idlh/67630.html) |

**Life-threatening range for an adult.** The three independent estimates converge on roughly **150–350 mL (5–12 fl oz) of 70% rubbing alcohol**, equivalent to about **1–2.7 g/kg** of isopropanol. A standard 16-fl-oz (473 mL) bottle exceeds this range. These figures describe untreated or late-presenting patients: StatPearls notes survival in adults above this range, and isopropanol ingestions overall are rarely lethal with supportive care ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/)). Tolerance in chronic alcohol users shifts the curve right; co-ingested sedatives or ethanol shift it left.

StatPearls does not state the concentration its 2–4 mL/kg figure refers to; it is conventionally read as 70% solution. Read as pure isopropanol it gives 110–220 g for a 70-kg adult, which brackets the NIOSH 190 g figure, so the practical range above holds either way.

**Bedside estimate of peak level (derived).** Using Vd 0.65 L/kg and assuming complete absorption with no elimination (an upper bound):

> Peak serum isopropanol (mg/dL) ≈ **85 × (mL/kg of 70% product ingested)**

So 2–4 mL/kg predicts roughly 170–340 mg/dL, consistent with the concentration bands below.

### 4.2 Serum concentration

| Serum isopropanol | Typical findings | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 50–100 mg/dL (8–17 mmol/L) | Inebriation, ataxia, vomiting† | Olson†; Slaughter 2014† |
| > 150 mg/dL (> 25 mmol/L) | Stupor to coma in non-tolerant patients† | Olson† |
| > 200–400 mg/dL (> 33–67 mmol/L) | Poor-prognosis band: hypotension, deep coma, respiratory depression | Case-review literature ([PMC10874632](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10874632/))‡ |
| ≥ 400 mg/dL (≥ 67 mmol/L) | Deaths reported; hemodialysis threshold in some sources | [PMC10874632](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10874632/)‡ |
| > 500 mg/dL (> 83 mmol/L) | Hemodialysis consideration threshold | [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/) |

Levels correlate imperfectly with outcome. **Hypotension is the strongest clinical predictor**: it marks severe overdose and has been associated with mortality near 45% ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/)). Manage the patient, not the number.

### 4.3 Children

- Systemic toxicity occurs at small absolute volumes, and by skin or inhalation. A 2-year-old sponged with about 4 oz of 70% isopropanol developed lethargy and vomiting with gastric bleeding and required PICU care, recovering after about 12 h of supportive treatment ([poison.org](https://www.poison.org/articles/rubbing-alcohol-only-looks-like-water)).
- Young children are at added risk of **hypoglycemia** because of limited hepatic glycogen ([CDC MMWR 2017](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/66/wr/mm6608a5.htm)).
- Among 70,669 hand-sanitizer exposures in children ≤ 12 y reported to US poison centers in 2011–2014, serious effects were rare (coma 5, seizures 3, hypoglycemia 2, metabolic acidosis 2, respiratory depression 2; no deaths); most symptomatic cases had only ocular irritation or vomiting ([CDC MMWR 2017](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/66/wr/mm6608a5.htm)).

### 4.4 Inhalation (occupational)

NIOSH REL 400 ppm TWA / 500 ppm STEL; OSHA PEL 400 ppm TWA; IDLH 2,000 ppm, set at 10% of the lower explosive limit rather than on toxicity. Volunteers reported mild-to-moderate eye, nose and throat irritation at 400–800 ppm for 3–5 min ([CDC/NIOSH Pocket Guide](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/npg/npgd0359.html); [CDC/NIOSH IDLH](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/idlh/67630.html)).

## 5. Clinical presentation and progression

| Time after ingestion | Findings |
|---|---|
| **0–1 h** | Rapid inebriation, often deeper than expected for the volume; dizziness, headache, slurred speech, ataxia. Early GI irritation: nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain. Osmol gap is at its highest. |
| **1–6 h** (peak) | Maximal CNS depression: stupor, coma, loss of deep tendon and corneal reflexes; miosis is the most common pupillary finding. Hemorrhagic gastritis and hematemesis. In severe cases respiratory depression, hypothermia, hypotension, cardiopulmonary collapse. **Coma, if it is going to occur, is usually present by 6 h.** |
| **6–24 h** | Isopropanol falling, acetone rising: ketonemia/ketonuria, fruity (acetone) breath, lingering sedation. Spurious creatinine elevation appears. Aspiration and bleeding complications declare themselves. |
| **> 24 h** | Gradual clearing as acetone (t½ ~22 h) is eliminated. Recovery is usually complete. |

Sources: [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/); [PubChem/HSDB](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/3776); [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/002660.htm).

**Severity grading**

| Grade | Features |
|---|---|
| Mild | Inebriation, vomiting, normal vitals, protects airway |
| Moderate | Stupor, persistent vomiting or hematemesis, tachycardia, mild hypothermia |
| Severe | Coma, respiratory depression, **hypotension**, hypothermia, significant GI bleeding |

Death, when it occurs, is from respiratory arrest or refractory shock ([PubChem/HSDB](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/3776)).

## 6. Laboratory evaluation

**Order:** glucose (bedside, immediately), basic metabolic panel, serum osmolality, serum or urine ketones, lactate, ethanol, CBC, transaminases, acetaminophen and salicylate levels, pregnancy test where relevant, ECG ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/)). Send serum isopropanol and acetone (gas chromatography) where available, but do not delay management for the result.

**Characteristic pattern**

| Finding | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Elevated osmol gap** | Early, before metabolism. Each 1 mg/dL of isopropanol adds ~0.17 mOsm/kg, so estimated level (mg/dL) ≈ osmol gap × 6. Acetone also contributes. ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/)) |
| **Ketosis without acidosis** | Positive serum/urine ketones, normal anion gap, normal bicarbonate, normal glucose ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/)) |
| **Falsely elevated creatinine** | Acetone interferes with colorimetric creatinine assays†: about +1 mg/dL creatinine per 100 mg/dL acetone, with a **normal BUN**. Acetone (mg/dL) ≈ (measured − baseline creatinine) × 100. Do not mistake this for acute kidney injury. ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/)) |
| Hypoglycemia | Uncommon in adults; check in every child and every malnourished or chronic-alcohol patient ([CDC MMWR 2017](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/66/wr/mm6608a5.htm); [PubChem/HSDB](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/3776)) |

**Red flag: an anion-gap metabolic acidosis.** Isopropanol does not produce one. Consider:

- Methanol or ethylene glycol, alone or co-ingested. Hand sanitizers contaminated with methanol have caused serious poisonings and deaths ([CDC MMWR 2020](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/69/wr/mm6932e1.htm)); a product label reading "alcohol" does not exclude it.
- Lactic acidosis from hypotension or seizures, which itself signals severe poisoning.
- Diabetic, alcoholic or starvation ketoacidosis; salicylates ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/)).

## 7. Triage and treatment thresholds

The dose cut-offs in 7.1 and 7.2 are **proposed operational thresholds** derived from the dose–response data in section 4. The primary sources give lethal-dose and observation-time data but not a published home-triage algorithm; these cut-offs need medical-director sign-off before adoption.

### 7.1 Observe at home (with poison-center follow-up)

All of the following:

- Unintentional exposure
- Taste, lick or single small sip; in a child, estimated dose clearly below ~0.5 mL/kg of 70% product†
- Asymptomatic, or only transient mouth/throat irritation or a single episode of vomiting
- Reliable observer; no co-ingestants

Advice: rinse mouth, give a small drink of water or milk if swallowing normally, **do not induce vomiting** ([MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/002660.htm); [PubChem/HSDB](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/3776)). Give children a carbohydrate-containing snack or drink. Call back at 1 h and again at 2–3 h, covering the 30 min – 3 h window in which serum levels peak ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/)). Dermal exposure: remove soaked clothing, wash skin, ventilate the room. Eye exposure: irrigate for at least 15 min ([MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/002660.htm)).

### 7.2 Refer to the emergency department

Any one of:

- **Intentional** ingestion (self-harm or ethanol substitution), regardless of symptoms
- **Any CNS effect:** drowsiness, ataxia, slurred speech, confusion
- Persistent vomiting, abdominal pain, or any hematemesis
- Estimated dose **≥ 0.5 mL/kg of 70% product**† or unknown amount in a child; more than a mouthful in an adult
- Child with significant dermal/inhalational exposure (alcohol sponging, soaked dressings) and any symptoms
- Co-ingestants, or a product of uncertain composition (possible methanol)
- Unreliable history or no competent observer

**Send by EMS** for depressed consciousness, abnormal breathing, hypotension, seizure, or reported ingestion approaching 2 mL/kg.

### 7.3 Emergency department management

1. **Airway and breathing.** Intubate for coma with loss of airway reflexes or respiratory depression. Aspiration is the main preventable complication.
2. **Circulation.** IV crystalloid for hypotension; vasopressors if fluid-unresponsive. Persistent hypotension is the trigger to call nephrology (section 7.4).
3. **Glucose.** Bedside check on arrival; treat hypoglycemia with IV dextrose ([PubChem/HSDB](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/3776)).
4. **Decontamination: none routinely.** Gastric emptying and activated charcoal are not recommended because absorption is rapid ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/)). Decontaminate skin if dermally exposed.
5. **No ADH blockade.** Fomepizole and ethanol are **contraindicated for isolated isopropanol**: the metabolite causes neither acidosis nor end-organ injury, and blocking ADH prolongs intoxication ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/)). Give fomepizole only if methanol or ethylene glycol co-ingestion is suspected (anion-gap acidosis, unexplained history).
6. **GI bleeding.** Acid suppression and standard management of hemorrhagic gastritis; transfuse as indicated.
7. **Adjuncts.** Rewarm if hypothermic. Thiamine, folate and multivitamin in malnourished or chronic-alcohol patients ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/)).

**Disposition**

| Situation | Disposition |
|---|---|
| Asymptomatic, unintentional, small ingestion; normal vitals and glucose after observation through the absorption peak (to ~3 h post-ingestion; many centers use 2–4 h†) | Discharge with return precautions |
| Symptomatic | Observe **at least 6–12 h** and until clinically sober ([PubChem/HSDB](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/3776) (Olson); [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/)) |
| Coma, intubation, hypotension, GI hemorrhage, level > 200 mg/dL | ICU |
| Intentional self-harm | Psychiatric evaluation once sober |

### 7.4 When hemodialysis is considered

Hemodialysis removes both isopropanol and acetone efficiently, but **is rarely needed**: most patients, including many who are comatose, recover with airway support and fluids alone ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/)).

Consult nephrology and the poison-center toxicologist for:

| Indication | Source |
|---|---|
| **Hypotension persisting despite fluids** (or requiring vasopressors) | [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/); [PMC10874632](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10874632/)‡ |
| **Lactic acidosis** | [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/) |
| **Serum isopropanol > 400–500 mg/dL** (> 67–83 mmol/L). StatPearls uses > 500 mg/dL; case-review literature uses ≥ 400 mg/dL | [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/); [PMC10874632](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10874632/)‡ |
| Deep coma or respiratory failure requiring intubation, particularly with a rising level or failure to improve | [PMC10874632](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10874632/)‡ |

Supporting considerations: impaired renal function (slower acetone clearance) and co-ingested methanol or ethylene glycol, which carry their own dialysis indications.

Not indications on their own: an elevated osmol gap, ketosis, the spurious creatinine rise, or uncomplicated coma in a patient with stable hemodynamics and a secured airway. A patient not comatose by 6 h post-ingestion is unlikely to need dialysis ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK493181/)).

## 8. Pitfalls

- Treating reflexively as "toxic alcohol" and giving fomepizole.
- Reading the acetone-induced creatinine rise as renal failure (check BUN).
- Attributing an anion-gap acidosis to isopropanol and missing methanol, ethylene glycol or shock.
- Mistaking the picture for DKA: ketones are positive, but glucose and pH are normal.
- Discharging while still sedated; acetone prolongs impairment after the isopropanol level has fallen.
- Missing dermal exposure in a lethargic febrile child: ask about alcohol sponging.
- Omitting a glucose check in a child.
